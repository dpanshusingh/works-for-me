# Planning a language from today to a deadline

## Shape

Four stages. Each produces something the learner uses, and the order is chosen so
each stage makes the next one cheaper.

1. **Script** — day one, and genuinely finishable in a day or two for an
   alphabet or abugida. Until someone can write the script, every other activity
   is mediated by transliteration, which they then have to unlearn. This is what
   the trainer is for.
2. **Grammar and survival phrases** — the next few days. Core sentence patterns
   plus the specific things they will say this week.
3. **Vocabulary through real material** — ongoing from the first week. Frequency
   list for the floor, film and books for everything above it.
4. **The daily loop** — what carries them to the deadline, plus the test that
   says whether they got there.

## Stage 2: grammar worth teaching first

Teach the sentence, not the paradigm. The useful first pass is: word order, how
the verb changes for person and politeness, how to negate, how to ask a question,
and the handful of case or preposition markers that carry most sentences. Map each
one onto a language they already speak and name what is new. For a Hindi speaker
learning Bangla that means: same word order, no grammatical gender (a real
simplification, say so), suffixes instead of postpositions, three levels of "you",
classifiers on counted nouns.

Then the phrase sets, drawn from their actual week. Typically: bargaining,
directions, ordering food, introductions, and whatever their hobby is. Write these
in the register they will really use. Shouted football instructions are informal
and clipped; a market exchange is polite but firm. Giving someone a textbook-formal
sentence to shout on a pitch is giving them something they will not say.

## Stage 3: ladders, not lists

Order material by difficulty and say why each rung sits where it does.

- **Listening.** Children's and genre films first: clean diction, simple plots,
  high redundancy. Then mainstream drama. Then art cinema and anything dialect-
  heavy. Contemporary speech for ear training, since that is what the street
  sounds like, is a different rung from classic cinema's careful enunciation.
- **Reading.** Verse for children first, because rhythm and rhyme carry a reader
  over unknown words and the script becomes automatic. Then short stories with a
  parallel translation. Then genre novels, which pull you forward. Literary and
  classical poetry last: the vocabulary is the densest in the language.

Name actual titles. A plan that says "watch films in the target language" has told
the learner nothing they did not know.

## Stage 4: the loop and the test

A loop that survives a bad day: roughly twenty minutes of drills, half an hour of
listening at natural speed, twenty minutes of speaking out loud (shadowing a scene
counts, and a voice note to a friend counts double). Weekly: a page of handwriting,
a film, a story, one real conversation with a person.

Then one concrete test per milestone, phrased so the answer is yes or no. For a
three-month "live my day in it" target: hold a fifteen-minute conversation about
your week, read a newspaper front page, write a paragraph by hand. Milestones a
learner can check themselves are what keep a plan alive after the first fortnight.
